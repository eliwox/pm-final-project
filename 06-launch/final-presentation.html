Income explainer

For consumers on shift or variable income, we explain which income to enter, why it’s asked and how it’s kept, right beside the field, so they can answer with confidence and set up a plan they can keep.

Slide 5 · Strategy

The problem, the value and the hypothesis

Problem statement hook

We must solve the risk that a rising Self-Service Rate masks failing plans, flat recovery and eroding client and portfolio value by addressing Mara’s struggle to find out whether an instalment plan is affordable and safe, which pushes her to abandon the portal, accept plans she will miss, or call Daniel, who must then redo the work.

Value proposition

The income explainer tells a consumer on shift or variable income which income to enter, why it’s asked and how her answer is used and kept, beside the field where she stalls today. It matters because variable and shift earners (2,862 starters a month) miss payments within 60 days at 22.5%, against 17.4% for fixed salary, and “why income is asked” is 16.2% of agent contacts.

Data-backed hypothesis

I believe that the inline income explainer will result in fewer plans that break within 60 days, as measured by a 4-point reduction (22.5% to 18.5%) in the 60-day missed-payment rate for variable or shift agreements, with a read at about 4.7 months. We will protect agreement rate (baseline 41.6%) throughout the test.

Slide 6 · Research

Competitive analysis: what she does when the journey fails her

Persona

A consumer on shift or variable income, with one or more open claims, trying to settle what she owes through the digital portal. Goal: to settle what she owes with a plan she can afford and keep, without making her situation worse.

External tools (the workaround)

Confirmed outside channel: a phone call to an agent (N-04, N-08). Otherwise, her own memory and guesswork, since she has no calculator or explainer in the journey (N-01, N-02).

The process (confirmed path)
  1. She opens the portal on a break, on her phone, and starts the instalment journey (N-01).
  2. She reaches the income question and can’t tell whether it means this month or a normal month (N-01).
  3. She closes the portal “to come back” (N-01) or chooses the shortest plan as the “right answer” without checking affordability (N-02).
  4. Unsure about consequences, she phones an agent (N-04).
  5. The agent asks her for the same details she already typed, because he can’t see them (N-08, T-205).
Core frustration

The income question. It is where the journey asks for sensitive information without explaining which income is meant, why it’s needed or how it will be used (N-01, T-206). It is also where the fear of getting it wrong appears (N-06).

The evidence
“It asked for my income and I did not know if they meant this month or a normal month. I closed it and meant to come back.”N-01 · confusion
“I picked the plan with the lowest number of months because it looked like the right answer. The amount was too high and I missed the second payment.”N-02 · guessing
“I was scared to type the wrong number. What if they use it against me?”N-06 · fear
“A person can tell me if this is going to cost me more.”N-04 · calling

In the data: “why income is asked” is 16.2% of agent contacts and “what if I miss a payment” is 18.6%. Variable or shift earners miss payments at 22.5% versus 17.4% for fixed salary.

PRD highlights

The “Now” feature

Income explainer: three plain statements beside the income field. Which income to enter, why it’s asked, and how the answer is used and kept.

Prototype learning

The first prototype came out entirely in German; it needed to be in English, and the version in the repo is.

Finalized Must-Haves

  1. Plain statement of which income to enter, at the point of entry.
  2. One-sentence reason the income is asked.
  3. Inline placement, in German, readable on mobile, with no navigation away from the question.

Demoted from Must

Slide 8 · Validation

Experiment plan

Hypothesis

I believe that the inline income explainer will result in fewer plans that break within 60 days, as measured by a 4-point reduction (22.5% to 18.5%) in the 60-day missed-payment rate for variable or shift agreements, with a read at about 4.7 months. We will protect agreement rate (baseline 41.6%) throughout the test.

Control (A)

The current income step: no statement of which income is meant (N-01), why it’s asked or how it’s kept (T-206), or how data is used (N-06).

Variant (B) · one change

The same step plus three short statements beside the existing income field, each 25 words or fewer: which income to enter, why it’s asked, and how the answer is used and kept. Nothing else changes.

22.5%Primary metric baseline: 60-day missed-payment rate, variable or shift agreements
4 ptsMDE: 22.5% down to 18.5%
1,598Agreements per arm · 50/50 · randomized by consumer
≈ 4.7 mo2.7 months enrolment plus 60-day follow-up
p < 0.05Two-sided, 80% power
Guardrail

Agreement rate, baseline 41.6%. Tripwire at 40.6% (1 point below control), checked weekly.

Isolation check · held constant

App version and release; eligibility, outreach and entry channels; the income field and its validation; the affordability questions and income document step (including T-201); the plan screen, its ordering and all amounts; chat, agent screens and reminders (T-207); the segment definition; no other test running on the plan screen or chat.

Shipping criteria
ShipMissed-payment rate is 4.0 points or more lower (to 18.5% or better), p < 0.05, and agreement rate is no more than 1.0 point below control.
HoldAgreement rate is 1.0 point or more below control at any weekly check, whatever the primary result.
IterateA significant improvement of 1.0 to 3.9 points. Pair the explainer with the affordability check and retest.
InconclusiveNot significant and under 4 points. If a diagnostic hits its pre-set threshold, iterate; if none does, drop it.
StopMissed payments are 1.0 point or more higher in the variant, or agreement rate is clearly lower with no offsetting gain.

Slide 9 · Launch

Go-to-market and success dashboard

GTM strategy
Goal · Conversion Tier · S, Minimal

Why conversion: consumers already reach the portal (8,360 starters a month), so awareness isn’t the gap. The feature exists to move a business metric: fewer plans that break within 60 days, which is 22.5% for variable or shift earners against 17.4% for fixed salary.

Audience: primary, consumers on shift or variable income with one or more open claims who start an instalment journey in the German portal, mostly on mobile (2,862 starters a month, 34% of all starters; about 1,191 agreements). Secondary, collection agents in the pilot team, who field the “why is income asked?” contacts (16.2% of agent contacts).

Why tier S: the feature is copy inside an existing step, so the audience is already in the portal and needs no announcement. The real risk is agents who keep re-asking the same questions, so the only investment is internal enablement. A louder launch could also draw attention to data use, which N-06 shows already worries consumers.

Channels
Owned · The income step Owned · Collection agents Owned · Client account managers

The income step itself: the three plain statements shown beside the income field, where the misery happens. It reaches every variable or shift starter, 78% of them on mobile.

Collection agents: a briefing and one-pager so they point callers to the explainer instead of re-explaining.

Client account managers: a short note for client reporting, since clients care about complaints and broken plans (N-10). No earned or paid channel: paid promotion of collection outreach to financially stretched consumers would be inappropriate and unneeded.

Enablement and assets

Compliance-approved German copy deck, including the retention period. Agent one-pager and FAQ. A client account manager note. A check that the chat assistant’s answers about income don’t contradict the explainer wording. Support briefed, no extra budget.

Bad signal to watch for

Agreement rate falls to 40.6% or lower while missed payments improve. The explainer may be deterring people instead of helping them.

Likely post-launch decision

Iterate. A copy-only change will probably land under the 4-point target (power is about 55% at 3 points and 28% at 2), so a significant 1 to 3.9 point improvement triggers pairing with the affordability check and a retest. Ship if the drop is 4 points or more, p < 0.05, and the guardrail holds.

Slide 10 · Story

Individual insights

Friction points and the aha moment

The hardest part was keeping the evidence honest. Several ideas I liked had no support in the interviews, such as the pay-day date, and the shortest-plan-first feature looked cheap but the data pointed the other way.

The second surprise was the experiment math. A 2-point improvement needed 6,623 agreements per arm, which meant about 11 months of enrolment, so I had to choose a larger effect to detect and accept a less decisive test.

The aha moment was seeing that the biggest drop-off wasn’t where I had looked first: only 22% of portal entrants start a journey at all.

Key takeaways and what I’d do next

Takeaways

  1. A rising self-service rate can hide failing plans, because a plan counts as resolved the day it is created.
  2. The explainer alone probably won’t deliver a 4-point drop, so it should be paired with the affordability check.
  3. A test needs a realistic sample size before it needs a clever feature.

Next

Get compliance sign-off on the copy, confirm how the variable-income segment is assigned, pre-set thresholds for the secondary metrics, and start the A/B test. In parallel, start discovery on combining claims, which has the largest segment gap (agreement 52.1% at one claim vs 27.6% at three or more).
