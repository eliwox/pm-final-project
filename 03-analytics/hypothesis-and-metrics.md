# Hypothesis & Success Metrics (Module 3)

## Pre-work · Hypothesis check
- **Role , who you are solving for (from M2):** A consumer on shift or variable income, with one or more open claims, trying to settle what she owes through the German portal, mostly on her phone.
- **Goal , what this user is ultimately trying to achieve:** To settle what she owes with a plan she can afford and keep, without making her situation worse.
- **Friction / moment of misery , the specific pain blocking their goal:** At the income question, Mara can’t tell whether “income” means this month or a normal month , why it’s asked, or how it’s used, and she fears a wrong number could be used against her. Unable to see what happens if she misses a payment, she picks the plan that “looked like the right answer” and misses the second payment (N-02), or closes the portal and doesn’t return. With two claims, she can’t tell whether paying one stops letters about the other.
- **Current workaround , the external tool or manual process they rely on (M2):** Confirmed by the research: she pauses and plans to come back, picks the shortest plan as a shortcut, phones an agent, or asks the chat for a quick yes. The agent then re-asks details she already typed. Hypothesis, not in the data: she also uses Google, a notes-app budget, or debt advisers.
- **Problem Hook , your one-sentence framing of the business crisis (M1):** We must solve the risk that a rising Self-Service Rate masks failing plans, flat recovery and eroding client and portfolio value, by addressing Mara’s struggle to find out whether an instalment plan is affordable and safe, which pushes her to abandon the portal, accept plans she will miss, or call Daniel, who must then redo the work.
- **Value Proposition , the outcome your initiative promised to deliver (M1):** For consumers like Mara and agents like Daniel, we will deliver a digital instalment journey that helps people agree to an affordable, understandable plan they can keep, with a clear handoff to an agent when they need one. Every month without it adds missed plans, repeated agent checks and weaker client and portfolio recovery.

## Read the evidence in your case
- **Where does your case say people stall or leave? Does that match the friction you wrote in Module 2?:** What the data shows. Of 100,000 outreach recipients, 38,000 enter the portal (38%), and only 8,360 start a journey (22% of entrants, 8.4% of outreach). Inside the journey, 3,846 of 8,360 starters reach an agreement (46.0%). Of those who reach agreement, 81.0% (3,115) have no missed payment within 60 days.

Confirms: The biggest in-journey losses are between the affordability questions and the document step (19.3%, about 1,421 people) and between the document step and plan review (22.1%, about 1,313 people). That is the income area where I placed the friction. T-201 says 853 journeys asked for a document that never arrived, which could explain much of the second loss, but the pack doesn’t link the two.

Adds nuance: The largest drop of all happens before the journey. About 29,640 portal entrants never start (78%), and about 62,000 outreach recipients never enter. Module 2 says nothing about these people.

Can’t separate: The funnel can’t tell the income question itself from the document upload, so I can’t pin the friction to the question wording specifically.

Missing metric: Step-level abandonment reasons, and page-level exits for the income question versus the upload.
- **What got better or worse in the case, and does that support the workaround your persona uses?:** What the data shows. Over six months, agreement rate rose from 43.1% to 46.0%, agent contact within 7 days fell from 33.4% to 31.0%, and Self-Service Rate rose from 31.1% to 34.7%. Missed payments within 60 days rose from 15.2% to 19.0%. The jump came in Month 4, when the 6-month plan became the first option: missed payments went from 15.6% to 18.4%, while agreement rate went from 44.2% to 45.6%.

Does it support the workaround?

Supports: The headline metrics improved while plan quality worsened, which matches “a rising Self-Service Rate masks failing plans.” Agent contacts still run at about 31%, and 14.7% of contacts are “wants lower monthly amount,” consistent with plans that feel too high (N-02).

Contradicts or complicates: Agent contact is falling, so the “she calls a person” workaround is not growing. In absolute terms, agreements kept for 60 days rose from about 2,964 (Month 1) to 3,115 (Month 6), roughly 5%. The plan-order change may therefore have helped on net, so the story is not simply “it got worse.”

Can’t prove: The Month 4 timing is an overlap, not a cause. The pack doesn’t show which plan length consumers actually chose, so the “shortest plan as the right answer” shortcut (N-02) is untested.

Missing metrics: Plan length chosen by month, recovered amount per agreement, and re-contact after a plan breaks.
- **Which user in the case is carrying the pain, and is that the person your Module 2 persona describes?:** Variable or shift earners (Mara’s income pattern). They make up 2,862 starters (34%) and fare worse than fixed-salary consumers:

Metric	Variable or shift	Fixed salary
Agreement rate	41.6%	48.1%
Missed payments	22.5%	17.4%
Self-service resolution	31.3%	36.5%

Multi-claim consumers. They show a larger gap, and it appears at a different point:

Open claims	Agreement rate	Agent contact	Missed payments
1	52.1%	25.6%	18.1%
2	37.7%	40.0%	21.4%
3+	27.6%	43.0%	21.2%

The 2,968 multi-claim starters are 35.5% of the total. Median instalments also rise with claims (€58.72, €88.73, €114.84), which may point to affordability pressure but is only a hypothesis.

Does it match the persona? Mostly, with one correction. Mara combines two pains that the data separates: multi-claim consumers mostly fail to get an agreement, while variable earners mostly get plans that fail. The pack has no cross-tab of income pattern by open claims, so I can’t tell how much they overlap.

Other signals pointing in different directions:

Mobile: 6,563 starters (78%) use mobile and agree less often than desktop (43.8% vs 54.0%), but their missed-payment rates are almost the same (18.9% vs 19.2%). Mobile hurts getting to an agreement, not keeping it.
Chat: Chat users agree more often (53.3% vs 44.4%) but miss more payments (21.6% vs 18.3%). They may simply differ from non-users.
Purchased portfolios: Agreement rates are nearly equal (46.4% vs 45.9%), so N-07 looks like a smaller issue than its quote suggests. T-203 shows 343 purchased cases with amount mismatches, but the segment gap is small.

Missing metric: Income pattern by open-claims cross-tab, so the persona can be sized properly.

## Step 2 · Craft your hypothesis
- **Qualitative evidence (from M2) , quote the specific friction / moment of misery for your persona:** “It asked for my income and I did not know if they meant this month or a normal month. I closed it and meant to come back.” (N-01)
“I picked the plan with the lowest number of months because it looked like the right answer. The amount was too high and I missed the second payment.” (N-02)
- **Quantitative evidence (from M3) , name the metric or data point that confirms the pain; cite the number:** Variable or shift earners miss payments within 60 days at 22.5%, versus 17.4% for fixed salary, and agree less often (41.6% vs 48.1%).
Overall, missed payments rose from 15.2% (Month 1) to 19.0% (Month 6), while Self-Service Rate rose from 31.1% to 34.7%.
“What if I miss a payment” (482 contacts, 18.6%) and “why income is asked” (419 contacts, 16.2%) are two of the top three agent contact reasons.
Caveat: these are one-month associations, and the Month 4 plan-order change overlaps in time but isn’t proven as the cause.
- **Persona , role, goal, and the friction you confirmed in the reconciliation steps:** Role: A consumer on shift or variable income, with one or more open claims, using the German portal, mostly on mobile.
Goal: To settle what she owes with a plan she can afford and keep, without making her situation worse.
Confirmed friction: The unexplained income question and the hidden consequence of a missed payment, supported by the segment gap above. The multi-claim pain is also real (agreement rate 52.1% for one claim, 27.6% for three or more), but it’s a separate failure mode, and the pack has no cross-tab to show how much the two overlap.
- **Problem you are solving , one sentence describing the specific friction this initiative removes:** Variable-income consumers can’t tell what income the portal wants or what a missed payment would cost, so they guess a plan that breaks, or leave.
- **Strategic outcome , what behaviour change do you expect, and how does it map to retention / revenue / churn?:** Expected behavior change: More variable-income consumers choose a plan that fits their income, and fewer of those plans break within 60 days.
Retention: Plans kept for 60 days rise from 77.5% for this segment (100% minus 22.5%).
Churn: Fewer abandoned journeys and broken plans.
Revenue: Recovery should improve, but the pack has no recovery metric, so this is a hypothesis. I’d ask for recovered amount per agreement.
- **Primary success metric (initiative signal) , the leading indicator that tells you the gap is closing:** Missed-payment rate within 60 days for variable or shift agreements. The baseline is 22.5%, and the gap is closing as it moves toward the fixed-salary level of 17.4%.
- **Guardrail metric (product signal) , the metric that must NOT drop; it protects your existing base:** Agreement rate must not fall below its baseline: 41.6% for the variable segment and 46.0% overall. Otherwise the initiative could “improve” plan quality simply by turning people away. A secondary watch: agent contact within 7 days should stay at or below 31.0%.
- **Decision window , how much time or data before you scale, pivot, or kill? minimum threshold to proceed?:** Pilot: The variable or shift segment, about 2,862 starters a month (roughly 1,190 agreements at current rates). The missed-payment metric needs 60 days after each agreement, so the first full read comes about 3 months after launch.
Scale if the missed-payment rate drops at least 2 points (22.5% to 20.5% or lower) and agreement rate stays within 1 point of 41.6%. A 2-point move is larger than the 0.7-point range seen in Months 1 to 3 (15.2% to 15.9%), though that is overall data and the pack has no segment-level month-to-month spread.
Pivot if agreement rate falls more than 2 points, or missed payments don’t move after two full cohorts.
Kill if both metrics worsen across two cohorts.
Assumption: I assumed the pilot can be run as a controlled comparison. Without a control group, I’d treat any improvement as suggestive, given the overlapping Month 4 change.
- **Draft your full hypothesis sentence , one to three sentences; quote the metric, name the persona, name the outcome:** Based on the interview evidence that variable-income consumers can’t tell which income the portal wants or what a missed payment costs (N-01, N-02), and the data showing they miss payments within 60 days at 22.5% versus 17.4% for fixed-salary consumers, I believe that explaining the income question and showing missed-payment consequences before plan selection for consumers on shift or variable income with open claims will result in more plans they can afford and keep, as measured by a 2-point reduction (22.5% to 20.5% or lower) in the 60-day missed-payment rate for this segment. I will protect the segment’s agreement rate (baseline 41.6%, no drop of more than 1 point) and will make a go/no-go decision after two full cohorts, about 3 months after launch.
